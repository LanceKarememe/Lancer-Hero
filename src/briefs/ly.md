# Lymphoid Organs High-Yield Tables (Exam 3)

Lymphoid Organs (Djakiew) was 25/43 on your September quiz, with the thymus the weakest part at 2/10. Page numbers are pages of the combined PDF: slides pp. 508–560, handout pp. 561–578. Everything is from those pages unless labeled (outside the slides).

## 1. Organization of the lymphoid system (LO 1)

There are two central organs and everything else is peripheral; mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) houses 85% of all lymphoid tissue (pp. 510, 561).

| Class | Members | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central organ | Thymus | Makes T cells |
| Central organ | Bone marrow | Makes B cells |
| Peripheral, encapsulated | Lymph nodes | Surveys lymph |
| Peripheral, encapsulated | Spleen | Surveys blood |
| Peripheral, unencapsulated | MALT: single, diffuse, transitory, aggregated | Surveys mucosal surfaces |

The tissues in order of increasing complexity (p. 512):

1. Circulating lymphocytes
2. Diffuse lymphoid tissue
3. Solitary nodules
4. Aggregated nodules, incompletely encapsulated: tonsils, Peyer's patches, vermiform appendix
5. Specific organs, encapsulated: bone marrow, thymus, lymph nodes, spleen

| Circulating lymphocyte | Share of blood lymphocytes | Detail (pp. 515, 563) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T cells | 75% | CD4+ helper 60%, CD8+ suppressor/cytotoxic 30% |
| B cells | 10% |  |
| Null cells | 15% | Natural killer cells, stem cells |

Blood holds only about 2% of the body's lymphocytes, and 1–2% of the pool recirculates every hour (pp. 561, 563).

| Homing term | Meaning | Page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Homing | Lymphocyte integrins bind vascular addressins on high endothelial venules | 511, 561 |
| Naive lymphocytes | Migrate through all peripheral lymphoid tissues | 563 |
| Memory and effector lymphocytes | Restricted to tissue-selective areas; the only ones that enter skin, joints and lung | 563 |
| Ecotaxis | Segregation into B-cell and T-cell areas inside a lymphoid structure; controlled by chemokines | 563 |
| T-cell rich | Same as thymus dependent | 563 |

| Immune privileged sites | Why (p. 563) |
| --- | --- |
| Eye, testis, brain | Blood-tissue barriers |
|  | No efferent lymphatic drainage |
|  | Constitutive expression of Fas ligand |
|  | Reduced expression of MHC molecules |

MHC is the major histocompatibility complex. Lymphatics are absent from the central nervous system, bone marrow, eyeball, adrenal cortex and inner ear (p. 565).

Two more numbers he stresses: 70–80% of all plasma cells are in the intestines (p. 563), and lymph is one-way, so it does not circulate (p. 563). In evolution, loose unencapsulated tissue came first, encapsulated organs next, and bone last (p. 513).

## 2. Diffuse tissue, IgA and nodules (LO 3, 5)

Diffuse lymphoid tissue sits in the lamina propria of mucosae and its main product is immunoglobulin A (IgA) (pp. 517, 565).

| Diffuse lymphoid tissue | Detail | Page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | Lamina propria of respiratory, gastrointestinal and urogenital mucosa | 565 |
| Cells | Lymphocytes (mostly B), plasma cells, eosinophils | 517 |
| Intraepithelial lymphocytes | CD8 and gamma-delta T cells | 565 |
| Paneth cells | Epithelial cells in the crypts; secrete lysozyme | 517 |

How IgA reaches the lumen (pp. 518, 565):

1. Plasma cells in the lamina propria release IgA as a dimer
2. Epithelial cells add the secretory piece during transcytosis
3. The secretory piece protects the IgA against lysosomal degradation
4. The piece is clipped at the surface and the IgA dimer is released into the lumen

IgA prevents pathogens colonizing mucosal membranes and neutralizes toxins and viruses (p. 565).

| Nodule | What it contains | State | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Mainly naive B cells | Resting, not stimulated | 520, 565 |
| Secondary: mantle (cap) | Resting naive B cells; stains dark | Periphery of a stimulated nodule | 520, 565 |
| Secondary: germinal center | B cells, helper T cells, follicular dendritic cells, macrophages with tingible bodies; stains pale | Site of B-cell activation, proliferation and differentiation | 520, 565 |

Nodules are unencapsulated, B-cell rich, and lie in the mucosa and submucosa of the respiratory, gastrointestinal and urinary tracts and the vagina. A nodule is not a lymph node; nodes are encapsulated (pp. 520, 565).

| Germinal center zone | Cell | What happens there (p. 565) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dark zone | Centroblasts | Somatic hypermutation |
| Light zone | Centrocytes | Clonal selection: higher antigen affinity |

The other germinal center activities are clonal expansion, class (isotype) switching, and generating effector and memory cells. The B cell leaves toward the mantle as an immunoblast and becomes a plasma cell or a memory cell (p. 565).

## 4. Thymus (LO 3–5)

The thymus is the central organ where thymocytes mature into T cells, and 95% of developing thymocytes are destroyed there (pp. 537, 569).

| Thymus fact | Detail | Page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape and type | Bilobed, lymphoepithelial | 534, 569 |
| Position | Rests on the pericardium in the superior mediastinum | 534, 569 |
| Origin | Epithelium of the third pharyngeal pouch, plus lymphoid stem cells from the blood | 569 |
| Maturation time | About 3 weeks | 569 |
| Output | Mature T cells that are either CD4+ or CD8+ | 569 |

| Epithelial reticular cell job | Detail (pp. 536, 569) |
| --- | --- |
| Framework | Physical microframework of every lobule, cortex and medulla |
| Hormones | Secretes thymosin and interleukins that support T-cell development |
| Barrier | Helps form the blood-thymus barrier, in the cortex only |
| Epithelial features | Desmosomes, cytokeratin filaments, keratohyaline granules |

| Selection step | Also called | What happens | Who controls it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Positive selection | MHC restriction | Cells that respect self-MHC are expanded; MHC I selects CD8 cells, MHC II selects CD4 cells | Cortical epithelial cells presenting MHC |
| Negative selection | Self tolerance | Self-attacking T cells are removed by clonal deletion through apoptosis | Cortico-medullary antigen-presenting cells, such as dendritic cells, presenting self-antigen |

| Feature | Cortex | Medulla |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lymphocytes | Dense, small thymocytes | Fewer but larger, maturing |
| Direction of travel | Outer cortex toward medulla as they differentiate |  |
| Blood-thymus barrier | Present | Absent |
| Macrophages | Phagocytose dying lymphocytes (tingible bodies) |  |
| Hassall's corpuscle | Absent | Present |
| Page | 538, 569 | 539, 569 |

The blood-thymus barrier is continuous capillaries with a thick basal lamina, surrounded by epithelial reticular cells. It lets thymocytes proliferate free of foreign antigens (p. 569).

| Hassall's corpuscle | Detail (pp. 539, 569) |
| --- | --- |
| Found | Thymic medulla only; unique to the thymus |
| Derived from | Medullary epithelial reticular cells |
| Appearance | Keratinized |
| Function | Source of thymic epithelial cytokines |

| Involution type | Cause | What changes | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aging | Begins after puberty, when the thymus is at its greatest weight | Lymphocyte depletion and fatty infiltration; under 15% of maximum weight by age 50 | 541, 571 |
| Acute stress | Severe disease, through corticosteroid hormones | Rapid loss of cortical thymocytes | 571, 577 |

| Compartment | Location | With involution (p. 571) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thymopoietic | Intraepithelial: cortex and medulla | Decreases |
| Perivascular | Extraepithelial: subcapsular space | Increases, with fat and mature lymphocytes |

Immunosenescence is the immune decline of aging that thymic involution may contribute to (pp. 541, 571).

## 5. Lymph node (LO 3–5)

A lymph node filters lymph and holds T and B cells for antigen recognition (pp. 544, 571).

| Region | Main cell | Antigen-presenting cell | Other | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cortex: follicles | B cells | Follicular dendritic cells, in germinal centers | Primary and secondary follicles | 545, 571 |
| Paracortex (deep cortex) | T cells | Interdigitating dendritic cells | High endothelial venules are here | 545–546, 571 |
| Medulla | B cells |  | Plasma cells in medullary cords secrete antibody into efferent lymph | 545, 571 |

Lymph flows through the node in this order (p. 573):

1. Afferent lymphatics
2. Subcapsular sinus
3. Trabecular sinus
4. Medullary sinus
5. Efferent lymphatics

| Lymph node fact | Detail | Page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scaffold | Reticular cells and fibers; argyrophilic | 545 |
| Sinuses | Criss-crossed by reticular fibers, which slow the lymph so macrophages can phagocytose debris and microbes | 573 |
| Filtering | Removes 99% of lymph debris | 571 |
| High endothelial venule | Postcapillary venule with cuboidal endothelium; the main route of lymphocyte entry | 546, 573 |
| Stay | 6–16 hours unless the lymphocyte meets its antigen; it leaves by the efferent lymphatics | 573 |
| Number | More than 500 nodes | 543, 571 |
| Rich sites | Neck, mediastinum, retroperitoneum, base of the extremities | 543, 571 |
| After antigen | Can reach 15 times its size in 5–10 days | 577 |
| Cancer | Biopsied for metastasis; breast cancer in four or more nodes carried the highest 15-year risk of return | 543 |

## 6. Spleen (LO 3–5)

The spleen is to blood as the lymph nodes are to lymph (p. 573).

| Spleen fact | Detail | Page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size | Largest single mass of lymphatic tissue in the body | 573 |
| Position | Left upper quadrant of the abdomen | 573 |
| Blood supply | Arterial from the systemic circulation; venous drainage into the portal circulation | 573 |
| Capsule and trabeculae | Contain some smooth muscle and elastic fibers, allowing limited contraction and expansion | 573 |

| Function | Detail | Page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filters blood | Especially old and damaged red cells; also microbes | 548, 573 |
| Immune response | To blood-borne antigens | 548, 573 |
| Platelet storage | Up to 33% of all blood platelets | 573 |
| Hematopoiesis | Normal in the fetus; extramedullary in the adult | 548, 573 |

| Region | Made of | Role | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| White pulp: periarterial lymphatic sheath (PALS) | T cells around a central artery | T-cell area | 511, 575 |
| White pulp: lymphoid nodules | B cells | B-cell area | 575 |
| Marginal zone | Between white and red pulp | Sorts incoming blood cells, by radial branches of the central arteries | 575 |
| Red pulp: splenic cords (cords of Billroth) | Reticular cell and fiber meshwork | Red cell destruction; platelet and iron storage; plasma cells; hematopoietic stem cells | 550, 575 |
| Red pulp: splenic sinusoids | Endothelial cells separated by slits of 1–3 microns | Only deformable, healthy cells squeeze back into the blood | 553, 575 |

The white pulp is the analog of the lymph node cortex (p. 573).

Blood takes this path (p. 575):

1. Central artery
2. Penicillar arterioles
3. Splenic cords
4. Venous sinuses

| Circulation | Purpose | Speed | Share (pp. 552, 575) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open | Filtration | Slow | 90% |
| Closed | Nutrition | Fast | 10% |

## 7. Master tables: where the cells live and how to spot each organ (LO 2–4)

Every peripheral organ has the same split: nodules or follicles are B-cell areas, and the tissue around a vessel or deep to the follicles is the T-cell area.

| Organ | B-cell area | T-cell area | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lymph node | Cortical follicles; medulla | Paracortex | 545, 571 |
| Spleen | Lymphoid nodules of white pulp | Periarterial lymphatic sheath | 575 |
| Any nodule | Mantle (naive B cells) and germinal center | Helper T cells inside the germinal center | 520, 565 |
| Gut lamina propria | Lymphocytes (mostly B) and IgA plasma cells | Intraepithelial T cells | 517, 565 |
| Thymus |  | Whole organ: thymocytes in cortex, maturing T cells in medulla | 569 |
| Bone marrow | Central organ for B cells | Also holds plasma cells and T cells as a peripheral organ; a major source of serum antibody | 567 |

His ecotaxis slide adds the fluids: blood and thoracic duct lymph are mostly T cells, while mucosal tissues are mostly B cells (p. 511).

| Antigen-presenting cell | Where |
| --- | --- |
| Follicular dendritic cell | Germinal centers |
| Interdigitating dendritic cell | Paracortex of the lymph node |
| Dendritic cell | Cortico-medullary junction of the thymus, for negative selection |

| Organ | Capsule | Feature that identifies it | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Palatine tonsil | Incomplete | Crypts lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium | 523, 559 |
| Peyer's patch | None | Nodules under small-intestine villi | 525, 558 |
| Appendix | None | Ring of nodules below crypts; no villi | 528, 558 |
| Thymus | Complete, with trabeculae and lobules | Dark cortex, pale medulla, Hassall's corpuscles; no nodules | 536, 539 |
| Lymph node | Complete | Subcapsular sinus, cortical follicles, paracortex, medullary cords and sinuses | 545, 557 |
| Spleen | Complete, with trabeculae | White pulp around central arteries, set in red pulp; no cortex and medulla | 549–550 |

"No nodules" for the thymus and "no cortex and medulla" for the spleen are my shorthand from the slide images, not his wording.

## 8. Clinical context

These are the handout's clinical notes, each tied to the structure it tests (p. 577).

| Condition or fact | Mechanism | Structure it tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IgA in breast milk against gut antigens | IgA-committed cells home to distant MALT sites, including the lactating mammary gland | MALT homing |
| X-linked agammaglobulinemia | Block in pre-B to B cell maturation in bone marrow; no primary or secondary follicles | Bone marrow, follicles |
| Acute stress | Corticosteroids kill cortical thymocytes; thymus shrinks | Thymic cortex |
| DiGeorge syndrome | Third and fourth pharyngeal pouches fail; no thymus, no T cells | Thymus origin |
| Malaria | Infected red cells lose deformability and are filtered out | Splenic sinusoids |
| Splenectomy | Serious bacterial infections (mortality up to 50–80%); more circulating platelets and aged red cells | Spleen functions |
| Portal hypertension, as in cirrhosis | Blood congestion enlarges the spleen | Portal venous drainage |
| M-cell transport | Polio virus, vibrio, cryptosporidia and HIV cross here | Peyer's patch |
| AIDS | Peripheral CD4 count falls; follicular dendritic cell network degenerates | Germinal centers |
| MALT lymphoma | Spreads to other mucosal sites | MALT homing |
| Hodgkin's disease | Reed-Sternberg cells, from transformed germinal center B cells | Germinal centers |
| Lymph node that fails to destroy what it traps | Becomes a new focus of infection or cancer | Lymph node filtering |
| Fetal immunity | By week 20 the fetus can mount T and B cell responses | Peripheral organs |
