## 1. Anabolism, catabolism and the carriers that link them (LO 1)

Catabolism breaks nutrients down and charges adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and the reduced cofactors, and anabolism spends them to build macromolecules (p. 588).

**Table 1a. Catabolism versus anabolism (p. 588)**

| Feature | Catabolism | Anabolism |
|---|---|---|
| Starts with | Energy-containing nutrients | Precursor molecules |
| Starting molecules listed | Carbohydrates, fats, proteins | Amino acids, sugars, fatty acids, nitrogenous bases |
| Ends with | Energy-depleted end products | Cell macromolecules |
| End molecules listed | CO2, H2O, NH3 | Proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, nucleic acids |
| Chemical energy | Captured in the carriers | Spent from the carriers |
| Effect on ATP | Made from ADP + HPO4 2- | Used, giving ADP + HPO4 2- |
| Effect on cofactors | NAD+, NADP+, FAD become reduced | NADH, NADPH, FADH2 become oxidized |

## 2. Reversible versus irreversible reactions (LO 2)

A reaction near equilibrium follows substrate and product concentrations, and a reaction with a very negative free energy change needs a separate bypass to go backward (p. 592, p. 594).

**Table 2a. Reversible versus "irreversible" (p. 592, p. 594, p. 610)**

| Feature | Reversible | "Irreversible" |
|---|---|---|
| Free energy change as written | ΔG ± 0 | ΔG << 0 |
| In words | At or close to equilibrium | Highly favorable free energy change |
| Reaches equilibrium | Normally operates at or near it | Not likely to reach it |
| Direct reversal | Readily reversible | Not likely under normal physiologic conditions |
| More substrate (A, B) | Shifts toward products | Not stated |
| More product (P, Q) | Shifts toward substrates | Not stated |
| To run the pathway in reverse | Same reaction runs backward | Needs a separate bypass reaction |
| Regulation | Not stated | Often regulatory targets |
| Slide wording | "Readily REVERSIBLE" | "May be functionally IRREVERSIBLE" |
| Review slide | "Many reactions are reversible" | "Separate reactions are required to bypass" |

**Table 2b. Reversible example: glucose 6-phosphate (G6P) to fructose 6-phosphate (F6P) (p. 593)**

| Feature | What the slide says |
|---|---|
| Enzyme | Phosphohexose isomerase |
| Ion drawn on the arrow | Mg2+ |
| Standard free energy change | 1.7 kJ/mol |
| Direction in glycolysis | Net forward (G6P to F6P) |
| Direction in gluconeogenesis | Net reverse (F6P to G6P) |
| What sets the direction | Solely the relative concentration of G-6-P and F-6-P |

**Table 2c. Irreversible example: F6P and fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (p. 595)**

| Direction | Enzyme (diagram) | Reaction | Standard free energy change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forward | PFK1, phosphofructokinase-1 (outside the slides) | F6P + ATP gives fructose 1,6-bisphosphate + ADP | Minus 14.2 kJ/mol |
| Bypass | F-1,6-BPase, fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase (outside the slides) | Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate gives F6P + Pi | Minus 16.3 kJ/mol |
| Both at once | | Net loss of ATP to ADP + Pi (read from the slide title) | Futile cycle |

**Table 2d. Keeping opposing reactions apart (p. 595)**

| Point | What the slide says |
|---|---|
| Rule | Opposing reactions must be coordinately controlled |
| If they are not | A futile cycle results |
| Option 1 | Carried out in different cellular compartments |
| Option 2 | Differentially controlled by allosteric regulators |
| Regulator drawn | Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6-BP) |
| F2,6-BP on PFK1 | Activates (green arrow, diagram only) |
| F2,6-BP on F-1,6-BPase | Inhibits (red bar, diagram only) |
| Makes F2,6-BP | PFK2 (kinase), from F6P using ATP (diagram only) |
| Removes F2,6-BP | PFK2 (phosphatase), giving F6P + Pi (diagram only) |
| Switch between the two PFK2 forms | Protein kinase A (PKA) points to the phosphorylated phosphatase form (diagram only) |
| Fate of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate | (2) Pyruvate (diagram only) |

## 3. Substrate-level versus oxidative phosphorylation (LO 3)

Substrate-level phosphorylation hands a phosphate from a substrate straight to ADP, and oxidative phosphorylation uses electron transfer to build a H+ gradient that drives ATP synthesis (p. 598, p. 599).

**Table 3a. The two ways to make ATP (p. 598, p. 599)**

| Feature | Substrate-level phosphorylation | Oxidative phosphorylation |
|---|---|---|
| Phosphate comes from | A high-energy phosphorylated substrate | Pi |
| Example donor | Phosphoenolpyruvate | None |
| Phosphate goes to | ADP | ADP |
| Energy source | The substrate itself | H+ gradient built by electron transfer |
| Electron donors | None | NADH and FADH2 ("reduced intermediates") |
| Where it occurs | Glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle | Inner mitochondrial membrane (diagram) |
| Stoichiometry | Measurable, whole-number, between substrates and products | Lacks whole-number, between reduced intermediate and ATP |
| Enzyme drawn | "Enzyme" (not named) | ATP synthase |
| Oxygen | Not mentioned | O2 is reduced to H2O (diagram); "oxygen dependent" (p. 590) |

**Table 3b. Oxidative phosphorylation, in order (p. 599)**

| Step | Event |
|---|---|
| 1 | NADH and FADH2 pass electrons to carriers of increasing reduction potential |
| 2 | The favorable free energy change of electron transfer generates a H+ gradient |
| 3 | Release of the gradient powers ATP synthesis from ADP and Pi |

**Table 3c. Parts drawn on the oxidative phosphorylation diagram, in order (p. 599, diagram only)**

| Order | Part | What it is drawn doing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | NADH dehydrogenase | Takes electrons from NADH + H+ (the "electron donor"), leaving NAD+; H+ moves out |
| 2 | Ubiquinone (CoQ) | Carries electrons onward |
| 3 | Cytochrome b-c complex | H+ moves out |
| 4 | Cytochrome c | Carries electrons onward |
| 5 | Cytochrome oxidase complex | 2 H+ + 1/2 O2 gives H2O; H+ moves out |
| 6 | ATP synthase | H+ returns to the matrix; ADP + Pi gives ATP |

| Location label | What is there |
|---|---|
| Intermembrane space | H+ accumulates |
| Inner mitochondrial membrane | Carriers and ATP synthase |
| Mitochondrial matrix | NADH is oxidized, O2 is reduced, ATP is made |
